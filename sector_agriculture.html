29  Agriculture

Agriculture is unlike the other sectors CIMS represents: most of its greenhouse-gas emissions are not from burning fuel but from biological and chemical processes — methane (CH₄) from livestock digestion and manure, and nitrous oxide (N₂O) from soils — so the sub-model is built around those process emissions and the options that abate them, rather than around energy services. The emphasis, as elsewhere, is on the structure of the energy-service flow model — the process and service nodes, the technologies that compete at each, and the engineering ratios that link them. The tree is region-agnostic. Tellingly, the sector’s activity is measured directly in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent, signalling that emissions, not energy or output, are its organising variable.

29.1 Scope

CIMS represents the greenhouse-gas-emitting activities of agriculture and the on-farm energy that supports them: livestock enteric fermentation and manure management (the CH₄ sources), agricultural soils (the N₂O source, together with the land-management practices that affect it), farm tractors (mobile diesel equipment), and farm process and building heat. The driving variable is the level of agricultural activity, expressed in CO₂-equivalent terms.

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere to avoid double-counting: the manufacture of nitrogen fertiliser is in Chemical Products (ammonia synthesis); food and beverage processing is in Light Industrial; the biofuels that use agricultural feedstock are in Biodiesel and Ethanol (which carry their own Agricultural Input services); and the fuels burned on-farm are supplied by the energy-supply sub-models. This chapter covers the emissions and energy use of farming itself.

29.2 Real-world context

Agriculture is responsible for roughly 8% of Canada’s greenhouse-gas emissions, and the composition is distinctive: it is overwhelmingly non-CO₂. Enteric fermentation — the methane cattle and other ruminants produce as they digest forage — is the single largest source, around 46% of agricultural emissions; manure management (CH₄ and N₂O) adds about 27%; and agricultural soils emit N₂O, chiefly from nitrogen fertiliser and crop residues (National Farmers Union 2022). On-farm fuel use (tractors, grain drying, heating) is comparatively small and is mostly diesel and natural gas.

This composition shapes the decarbonisation problem. Because the emissions are biological, the levers are agronomic and dietary rather than purely technological: feed additives and rumen modification to cut enteric methane, manure management changes, nitrogen and tillage management to cut soil N₂O, and land-use practices (agroforestry, wetland restoration, reduced tillage) that store carbon in soils and biomass. The feed additive 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP), for example, consistently cuts enteric methane by around 30% on average (Kebreab et al. 2021). The CIMS structure mirrors this: it carries explicit CH₄ and N₂O process-emission factors on the livestock and soils nodes and competes the abatement practices against conventional ones, while representing the on-farm energy through a small set of tractor and heat technologies.

29.3 Model structure

The sector divides into a Product sub-tree — the emitting agricultural processes — and a Service sub-tree of the on-farm energy services. Figure 29.1 shows the structure. Each node carries a competition rule (see Chapter 4); the abatement options compete against conventional practice on a lifecycle-cost basis, with the carbon price (applied to CH₄ and N₂O on a CO₂-equivalent basis) as the main driver.

29.3.1 Products: the emitting processes

  • Enteric Fermentation and Manure Management — the livestock methane source. It competes conventional Livestock against Livestock + Vaccines + Rumen changes, a package of dietary and biological interventions (feed additives such as 3-NOP, methane vaccines, rumen modification) that cuts the enteric-CH₄ factor by roughly 60% in the data.
  • Soils — the cropland N₂O source, competing land-management practices that differ in their soil-N₂O intensity and carbon storage: Conventional Tillage, Minimal Tillage, and Zero Tillage (progressively lower N₂O), and Agroforestry and Wetland Restoration, which store carbon and can be net-sequestering.

29.3.2 Services: tractors and heat

  • Tractors — on-farm mobile equipment, competing standard and medium-efficiency diesel against a hydrogen option.
  • Heat — farm process and building heat (grain drying, livestock housing), competing standard and efficient natural-gas and electric units.

29.4 Decarbonisation technologies

Agricultural decarbonisation means abating CH₄ and N₂O and storing carbon in soils and biomass, with on-farm energy a secondary concern. The estimates in Table 29.2 are order-of-magnitude figures from public literature (National Farmers Union 2022; Kebreab et al. 2021), expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars. Because most options are practices rather than plant, costs are given as a cost (or saving) per tonne of CO₂-equivalent abated. They are indicative, for relative comparison rather than budgeting, and are separate from the values the model carries internally.

29.4.1 Cutting enteric and manure methane

The largest single source — enteric methane — is addressed by dietary and biological means: feed additives (notably 3-NOP), methane-suppressing vaccines, and rumen modification, represented in the model as the Livestock + Vaccines + Rumen changes package. Manure methane is best cut by anaerobic digestion, which captures the gas for energy, and by covered storage — high-value options the model does not yet separate from enteric emissions (see the TODO above).

29.4.2 Cutting soil N₂O and storing carbon

Soil N₂O falls with reduced and zero tillage and with better nitrogen management (right rate, source, timing, and placement — “4R” stewardship — and nitrification inhibitors). Agroforestry and wetland restoration go further by storing carbon in biomass and soils, and are among the few agricultural options that can be net-sequestering. The model competes the tillage and land-use practices directly at the Soils node.

29.4.3 On-farm energy

On-farm energy is a smaller lever: efficient and hydrogen tractors cut mobile-diesel CO₂, and electric and efficient farm heat cut heating emissions, drawing on the same low-carbon options as the other sectors.

29.5 Economics

Technology choice at every node follows the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4. Each practice carries a capital cost (often small), operating cost, an activity basis, any service requests, and availability and lifetime years; these are combined into a lifecycle cost and converted to market shares by the inverse-power choice rule.

Agricultural practices are discounted at about 30% in the current data. As elsewhere in CIMS this is a behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rate rather than a true financial cost of capital, capturing the payback horizons and adoption barriers that govern real farm decisions; CIMS applies it throughout the lifecycle-cost calculation, as set out in the discount-rate discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4. Heterogeneity (variance) parameters are set around 10–15.

The emissions accounting is the heart of this sub-model and is almost entirely non-CO₂: it carries explicit CH₄ factors on the enteric/manure node and N₂O factors on the soils node, applied per unit of activity and entering on a CO₂-equivalent basis at their high global-warming potentials. Some land-use practices (agroforestry, wetland restoration) can carry net-negative factors, representing carbon storage. Because the activity variable is itself in CO₂-equivalent, the carbon price acts very directly here: it makes the abatement packages — feed additives, reduced tillage, sequestering land use — cost-competitive against conventional practice, which is the model’s channel for agricultural mitigation.

29.6 Regional coverage and differences

All seven modelled regions — Alberta, the Atlantic region, British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario, Québec, and Saskatchewan — carry the agriculture structure. The mix of sources differs by region: the Prairie provinces (Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba) dominate both cattle (enteric and manure CH₄) and cropland (soil N₂O from extensive grain and oilseed production); Ontario and Québec have mixed livestock and cropping; and the Atlantic and British Columbia have smaller, more specialised agricultural sectors.

29.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The agriculture sub-model captures the sector’s dominant emissions — enteric and manure methane, and soil N₂O — and the main practice-based abatement levers, but it is deliberately coarse. Its principal limitations are the lumping of manure with enteric fermentation (hiding the cost-effective digester and storage options), the absence of explicit nitrogen-management options, and the simplified treatment of soil-carbon sequestration, whose permanence and measurement are genuinely difficult. The TODOs above record these priorities. Because agricultural emissions are largely non-CO₂ and biological, they are among the hardest in the whole model to abate deeply, which makes a faithful representation of the available practices especially valuable.
