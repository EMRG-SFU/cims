15  Hydrogen

Hydrogen production is one of CIMS’s energy-supply sub-models: it produces a versatile energy carrier and chemical feedstock that other sectors consume — in oil refining, ammonia and methanol synthesis, potentially in low-carbon steelmaking (direct reduction), and in transport. The emphasis is on the structure of the energy-service flow model: the production routes that compete, the services they draw on, and the engineering ratios that link them. The tree is region-agnostic. The defining feature of hydrogen is that the molecule is identical however it is made, but its emissions are entirely a function of the production route — the familiar “colours” of hydrogen — so the whole sub-model is essentially one competition among production technologies that differ enormously in carbon intensity.

15.1 Scope

CIMS represents the production of hydrogen by all the major routes — natural-gas reforming, coal and biomass gasification, water electrolysis, and ethanol reforming — at both centralised and distributed scale, together with the delivery infrastructure and a shared CCS service. The sub-model produces hydrogen as an output that flows to the consuming sectors; its demand is set by those sectors’ fuel-switching and feedstock choices and by policy, rather than by an exogenous production target.

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere: the end uses of hydrogen — as a refinery input (Petroleum Refining, Petroleum Crude), an ammonia/methanol feedstock (Chemical Products), a reductant in steel (Iron and Steel), or a transport fuel — are represented in those sectors. This chapter covers only how the hydrogen is made.

15.2 Real-world context

Almost all hydrogen made in Canada today is “grey” — produced by steam methane reforming (SMR) of natural gas without capture, mostly in Alberta to serve oil-sands upgrading, refining, and ammonia production. Grey hydrogen is the cheapest, at roughly CAD 1.5–2.5/kg, but emits on the order of 9–10 kg of CO₂ per kg of hydrogen (Natural Resources Canada 2024). Canada’s Hydrogen Strategy and a new investment tax credit (15–40% of capital, the top rate for the cleanest hydrogen) aim to shift production toward low-carbon routes (Natural Resources Canada 2024).

Two such routes are advancing. “Blue” hydrogen — SMR or gasification with carbon capture (CCS) — is the basis of large Alberta projects such as the Air Products net-zero hydrogen complex (1,500 t/day with ~3 Mt CO₂/yr captured) and Linde’s clean-hydrogen facility; it costs roughly CAD 2–3.5/kg (Air Products 2024; Natural Resources Canada 2024). “Green” hydrogen — electrolysis powered by low-carbon electricity — is the lowest-emission route and is attractive where power is clean and cheap (Québec and BC hydro, Atlantic wind), but at roughly CAD 4.8–6.3/kg today it remains the most expensive (Natural Resources Canada 2024). The CIMS structure represents this whole ladder as a single production competition, with explicit life-cycle emissions and a CCS service, so the model can shift the production mix from grey toward blue and green as costs and the carbon price evolve.

15.3 Model structure

The sub-model divides into a Product sub-tree — the hydrogen-production competition — and a Service sub-tree holding the delivery Infrastructure and the CCS service. Figure 15.1 shows the structure. The production node is a single tech-compete node (see Chapter 4); the incumbent in the current data is distributed SMR.

15.3.1 Product: the production competition

The Production node competes the full set of routes, each consuming a characteristic mix of fuel, feedstock, and electricity and (for the capture routes) requesting the CCS service:

  • Steam methane reforming (SMR) — centralised and distributed, and a centralised + CCS variant. NG-based; the distributed route is today’s incumbent. (Grey, and blue with CCS.)
  • Coal gasification + CCS — centralised; coal-based blue hydrogen.
  • Biomass gasification — centralised, and a + CCS variant. Renewable feedstock; with CCS it is net-negative (the captured CO₂ is biogenic).
  • Electrolysis — centralised and distributed; splits water using electricity, so its emissions track the grid (green where the grid is clean). Electricity-dominated, at roughly 0.2 GJ electricity per unit of hydrogen energy.
  • Ethanol reforming — distributed; reforms ethanol from the Ethanol sub-model.

The centralised versus distributed distinction matters because it trades production economies of scale against the Infrastructure (delivery, compression) cost: distributed production is made near the point of use and needs less delivery infrastructure, while centralised production is cheaper per unit but draws more heavily on the Infrastructure service.

15.3.2 Services: infrastructure and capture

  • Infrastructure — the delivery, compression, and storage needed to get hydrogen to users; every production route requests it (distributed routes less so).
  • CCS — the shared carbon-capture service, removing 90% of the CO₂ presented to it, requested by the SMR-, coal-, and biomass-with-CCS routes.

15.4 Decarbonisation technologies

Decarbonising hydrogen means shifting the production mix away from unabated SMR toward capture-equipped and electrolytic routes. Because the product is identical, this is a pure cost-and-carbon competition; Table 15.1 summarises maturity and Table 15.2 gives indicative costs.

The cost estimates in Table 15.2 are order-of-magnitude figures from public literature (Natural Resources Canada 2024; International Energy Agency 2019), expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars (US- and euro-denominated sources converted at roughly 1.37 CAD/USD and 1.48 CAD/EUR and adjusted to 2025). Levelised costs are per kilogram of hydrogen; electrolyser capital is per kilowatt of electrolyser. They are indicative, for relative comparison rather than budgeting, and are separate from the values the model carries internally.

15.4.1 Blue hydrogen: SMR and gasification with CCS

The nearest-term low-carbon route keeps the established reforming or gasification process but adds carbon capture, cutting CO₂ by around 90%. It is the basis of Canada’s largest announced clean-hydrogen capacity, in Alberta, where low-cost natural gas and viable CO₂ storage favour it (Air Products 2024). In the model this is the SMR centralised + CCS, coal gasification + CCS, and biomass gasification + CCS routes, each requesting the shared CCS service.

15.4.2 Green and net-negative hydrogen

Electrolysis produces hydrogen with no direct CO₂; its footprint is the carbon intensity of the electricity, so it is genuinely “green” on Canada’s hydro and wind power but only as clean as the grid elsewhere. It is the most expensive route today, dominated by electricity cost and electrolyser capital, both of which are falling. Biomass gasification with CCS is distinctive: because the carbon is biogenic, capturing it yields net-negative hydrogen, one of the few production routes anywhere that removes CO₂ from the atmosphere.

15.5 Economics

Technology choice at the production node follows the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4. Each route carries a capital cost, fixed operating and maintenance cost, an output basis, service requests for the fuel, feedstock, electricity, infrastructure, and capture it consumes, and availability and lifetime years; these are annualised, combined with service costs into a lifecycle cost, and converted to market shares by the inverse-power choice rule.

Hydrogen technologies are discounted at roughly 25% in the current data. As elsewhere in CIMS this is a behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rate rather than a true financial cost of capital, capturing the payback horizons and adoption barriers that govern real investment; CIMS applies it throughout the lifecycle-cost calculation, as set out in the discount-rate discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4. Heterogeneity (variance) parameters are set around 25.

The emissions accounting is life-cycle, carrying CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O factors, so the upstream methane and the combustion and process CO₂ of each route are represented; the CCS service applies a 90% removal factor to the routes that request it, which can drive biomass-gasification hydrogen net-negative. Because the routes share an identical product, the carbon price acts directly on the production mix: as it rises, it raises the effective cost of grey SMR (through its large CO₂ factor) relative to the blue and green routes, shifting market share toward them. This makes hydrogen one of the clearest illustrations in CIMS of a carbon-price-driven technology transition.

15.6 Regional coverage and differences

All seven modelled regions — Alberta, the Atlantic region, British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario, Québec, and Saskatchewan — carry the hydrogen structure, but the economic route differs sharply by region. Alberta and Saskatchewan, with cheap natural gas and CO₂ storage, favour blue hydrogen; Québec, British Columbia, and Manitoba, with abundant low-carbon hydro electricity, favour green electrolytic hydrogen; and the Atlantic region’s wind resource supports green production. The same competition therefore resolves to different winners in different regions, driven by local fuel and electricity prices.

15.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The hydrogen sub-model is compact but captures the essential structure of the production-route competition. Its main limitations are the loose coupling to the sectors that consume hydrogen, the absence of a turquoise (pyrolysis) route and of the investment tax credit, and reliance on inherited cost assumptions. The most important is the first: until the hydrogen demands in refining, ammonia, steel, and transport are explicitly sourced from this competition, the model can understate the system value of building clean-hydrogen supply.
