8  Coal Mining

Coal mining is one of CIMS’s energy-supply and extractive sub-models: it produces coal for metallurgical use (coking coal for steelmaking) and for thermal use (power and industrial heat). The emphasis, as in the other extractive sectors, is on the structure of the energy-service flow model — the production chain, the technologies that compete at each stage, and the engineering ratios that link them. The tree is region-agnostic. Coal mining has a distinctive emissions signature: alongside the diesel its mobile equipment burns, it releases fugitive methane liberated from the coal seam during extraction, and this coal-mine methane is the sector’s largest greenhouse-gas source — yet, as discussed below, the current model carries no technology to abate it.

8.1 Scope

CIMS represents the extraction, size reduction, and finishing of coal — from the mine face through crushing, milling, washing, and drying to a saleable product. The driving variable is coal output in tonnes. Both metallurgical (coking) coal and thermal coal are within scope; the chain includes a Metallurgical Finishing stage (washing, cleaning/drying, tailings) that prepares coking coal to the specification the steel industry needs.

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere to avoid double-counting: the combustion of thermal coal for power is in Electricity and for industrial heat in the relevant industrial chapters; the conversion of coking coal to coke and its use in ironmaking are in Iron and Steel; and other (non-coal) mining is in the Mining sector. This chapter covers the mining of the coal itself.

8.2 Real-world context

Canada produced about 42.6 Mt of coal in 2024, roughly two-thirds of it metallurgical (Natural Resources Canada 2025). The geography is concentrated in the West: British Columbia accounts for around 69% of tonnage (the Elk Valley coking-coal mines), Alberta about 19% (metallurgical and thermal), and Saskatchewan about 11% (lignite/thermal). The two coal types are on opposite trajectories. Thermal coal is in steep decline as Canada phases out unabated coal-fired electricity by 2030 and moves to end thermal-coal exports, while metallurgical coal — for which there is no current large-scale substitute in conventional steelmaking — has held up and is increasingly framed as an export commodity (Natural Resources Canada 2025). Canadian coal production has fallen about 31% over the past decade, almost all of it thermal.

The sector’s emissions come from two places. Diesel powers the trucks, shovels, and other mobile equipment, as at any large mine. More distinctively, methane trapped in the coal seam is released during extraction — fugitive coal-mine methane (CMM), much of it carried out in the ventilation air (VAM). Coal-mine methane is the dominant greenhouse-gas source from coal mining, on the order of 60% of the total, and mature technologies exist to drain and destroy it — gas drainage, and ventilation-air-methane oxidation that can destroy more than 98% of the methane in mine ventilation air (International Energy Agency 2023; CSIRO 2026). The CIMS structure carries the fugitive-methane factor on the extraction node and the diesel emissions on the mobile equipment, but — as noted below — it does not yet offer a methane-capture option.

8.3 Model structure

The sector divides into a Product sub-tree — the coal production chain — and a Service sub-tree of auxiliary energy services. Figure 8.1 shows the structure. Each node carries a competition rule (see Chapter 4); the production chain is a fixed-ratio sequence and the technology choices occur at the tech-compete leaves.

8.3.1 Products: the coal production chain

  • Raw Product — the mine itself: Extraction (competing diesel, biodiesel, and electric equipment), Mine Ventilation, and Transportation (diesel and biodiesel trucks, or conveyance). The Extraction node carries the fugitive-methane emission factor.
  • Size Reduced Product — Primary Crushing, Primary Milling, and Secondary Milling of the run-of-mine coal.
  • Metallurgical Finishing — preparing coking coal: Washing (wash mill), Cleaning (rotary, spray, or fluidized-bed drying, on coal, natural gas, or electricity), and Tailings Disposal (reclamation, with diesel, biodiesel, or electric options).

The master tree also defines Simplified Extraction and Simplified Production nodes — lower-resolution alternatives to the full chain for regions or runs where the detailed structure is not warranted.

8.3.2 Services

The services follow the standard pattern and are documented in their own chapters in the General Services part: Machine Drive (see Industrial Machine Drive Systems) carries the large motor loads (the model’s current files still use the older per-horsepower and separate compression/conveyance/pumping/ventilation structure), together with HVAC, Lighting, and the Methane Blend gaseous-fuel node.

8.4 Decarbonisation technologies

Coal mining’s emissions split into fugitive methane (the larger share) and diesel combustion in mobile equipment, with smaller process-heat (drying) and electricity loads. The decarbonisation levers therefore are methane capture, equipment electrification or fuel-switching, and efficiency. Table 8.1 summarises maturity and Table 8.2 gives indicative costs. A crucial caveat: the single largest lever — methane capture — is not yet represented in the model.

The estimates in Table 8.2 are order-of-magnitude figures from public literature (International Energy Agency 2023; Mungyeko Bisulandu et al. 2026; SRK Consulting 2024), expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars (US sources converted at roughly 1.37 CAD/USD and adjusted to 2025). They are indicative, for relative comparison rather than budgeting, and are separate from the values the model carries internally.

8.4.1 Capturing coal-mine methane

The most important lever is the one the model is missing. Because methane has a high global-warming potential and is released in large quantities during mining, draining and using it (where concentrations allow) and oxidising the dilute methane in ventilation air (VAM) together address the majority of the sector’s emissions, with mature technology that can destroy more than 98% of vent methane (CSIRO 2026; International Energy Agency 2023). The model currently applies the same fugitive-methane factor to every extraction option, with no capture alternative.

8.5 Economics

Technology choice at every tech-compete node follows the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4. Each technology carries a capital cost, fixed operating and maintenance cost, an output basis, service requests for the energy it consumes, and availability and lifetime years; these are annualised, combined with service costs into a lifecycle cost, and converted to market shares by the inverse-power choice rule.

Coal mining technologies are discounted at a high rate — 35% at most process nodes in the current data, with some auxiliary nodes at 50%. These values are behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rates rather than true financial costs of capital, capturing the short payback horizons and adoption barriers typical of resource industries; CIMS applies this rate throughout the lifecycle-cost calculation, as set out in the discount-rate discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4. Heterogeneity (variance) parameters are set around 10.

The emissions accounting combines combustion CO₂ from diesel and drying fuel with the distinctive fugitive CH₄ factor on extraction (entering on a CO₂-equivalent basis at methane’s high warming potential). Because there is no methane-capture option and no CCS service, the model’s only channels for emissions reduction are equipment electrification, fuel-switching to biodiesel, and efficiency — which reach the combustion emissions but leave the dominant fugitive methane untouched. This is the most consequential representational gap in the sub-model, and it means the model understates the abatement available to the sector.

8.6 Regional coverage and differences

Four regions carry coal mining — Alberta, the Atlantic region, British Columbia, and Saskatchewan — reflecting where Canadian coal is actually mined:

  • British Columbia dominates, with metallurgical (coking) coal from the Elk Valley; this production is export-oriented and durable.
  • Alberta has both metallurgical and thermal coal, with thermal declining as coal power is phased out.
  • Saskatchewan mines lignite/thermal coal, the most exposed to the power phase-out.
  • The Atlantic region has a small coal presence.

Ontario, Québec, and Manitoba are absent, consistent with the absence of coal mining there.

8.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The coal mining sub-model captures the production chain and its diesel and electricity loads in good detail, but it has one large gap and several smaller ones. The large gap is the absence of any coal-mine-methane abatement technology, which omits the sector’s single most important emissions lever. The smaller debts are the older auxiliary structure (to be migrated to MECS machine drive), the lack of an explicit metallurgical-versus-thermal split, and reliance on inherited cost data. The TODOs above record these priorities.
