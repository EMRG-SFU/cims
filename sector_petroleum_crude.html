10  Petroleum Crude

Crude oil and bitumen production is one of CIMS’s energy-supply sub-models, covering the upstream oil industry — conventional crude oil and, dominating both output and emissions in Canada, the oil sands (surface mining, in-situ recovery, and upgrading to synthetic crude). The emphasis is on the structure of the energy-service flow model: the production routes, the technologies that compete at each, and the energy services — above all steam — that drive them. The tree is region-agnostic. The defining feature of the sector is that the energy used to produce the oil — particularly the natural gas burned to raise steam for in-situ bitumen recovery — is itself a very large emissions source, so the sector’s decarbonisation is mostly about how that production energy is supplied and whether its CO₂ is captured.

10.1 Scope

CIMS represents the production of crude oil and bitumen: conventional light, medium, and heavy crude (onshore and offshore), and the oil sands in their three forms — surface mining, in-situ recovery, and upgrading of bitumen to synthetic crude oil. The driving variable is oil/bitumen output. The model carries the supporting energy services — steam, process hydrogen (for upgrading), industrial engines, and a CCS service.

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere: refining crude into fuels and products is in Petroleum Refining; natural gas production is in Natural Gas; the combustion of the refined fuels is in the demand sectors; and the hydrogen the sector uses can be drawn from the Hydrogen sub-model. This chapter covers getting the oil and bitumen out of the ground and to a refinery-ready state.

10.2 Real-world context

Canadian crude production is dominated by the Alberta oil sands, the largest source of both output and emissions in the sector, supplemented by conventional crude (Saskatchewan, Alberta) and offshore production in the Atlantic (Newfoundland). The oil sands come in two forms: surface mining and upgrading, which digs out near-surface bitumen and upgrades it to synthetic crude; and in-situ recovery, which uses steam to mobilise deeper bitumen and now rivals mining as the main production route.

The sector’s emissions profile is distinctive. For in-situ production, the single largest source is steam generation — natural gas burned to make the steam that lowers bitumen viscosity in steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD) and cyclic steam — so the carbon intensity of a barrel depends heavily on how much steam it needs and how that steam is produced ( 2025). Upgrading adds the emissions of coking, hydrotreating, and the hydrogen it consumes. The decarbonisation levers follow directly: solvent-assisted in-situ processes that cut steam use (with potential to lower intensity by up to ~50%), low-carbon steam (electric, hydrogen, nuclear, cogeneration), and carbon capture — the focus of the Pathways Alliance, whose proposed oil sands CCS network would be among the world’s largest (Pathways Alliance 2024; 2025). The CIMS structure captures all of these as competing technologies at the production and steam nodes.

10.3 Model structure

The sector divides into a Product sub-tree — the production routes — and a Service sub-tree of the energy services that supply them. Figure 10.1 shows the structure. Each node carries a competition rule (see Chapter 4).

10.3.1 Products: conventional crude and the oil sands

  • Light/Medium crude — Onshore and Offshore conventional production.
  • Heavy crude — conventional heavy oil.
  • Exploration — the upstream drilling and appraisal activity.
  • Bitumen — the oil sands, in three linked nodes:
    • In-Situ — competing SAGD, SAGD with enhanced leak detection (ELDAR), Cyclic Steam, Solvent, and Hybrid Thermal-Solvent recovery. These differ in steam intensity (and therefore CO₂) and in methane (the solvent and hybrid routes cut both).
    • Mining — surface mining (Existing and Improved).
    • Upgrading — converting bitumen to synthetic crude, competing conventional Upgrading, Efficient, ELDAR, and ELDAR + CCS routes.

10.3.2 Services: steam, hydrogen, engines, and capture

  • Steam — the most important service, because in-situ recovery is steam-intensive. It competes natural-gas, heavy-fuel-oil, and petcoke boilers (with CCS variants), natural-gas cogeneration (with and without CCS), and electric, hydrogen, and nuclear steam. The choice here largely determines an in-situ barrel’s carbon intensity.
  • Still Gas — steam raised from refinery/upgrader still gas, with boiler and cogeneration options (with and without CCS).
  • Hydrogen — for upgrading (can be sourced from the Hydrogen sub-model’s grey, blue, and green routes).
  • Industrial Engines — diesel, gasoline, and propane mobile equipment (mine trucks, and the like).
  • CCS — the shared capture service (90% capture), central to deep decarbonisation of the sector.

10.4 Decarbonisation technologies

Oil-sands and crude decarbonisation works through reducing steam demand (solvent processes), decarbonising the steam and process energy (electric, hydrogen, nuclear, cogeneration), capturing CO₂ (the Pathways CCS network), and controlling methane. The estimates in Table 10.2 are order-of-magnitude figures from public Canadian sources (Pathways Alliance 2024; 2025), expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars. They are indicative, for relative comparison rather than budgeting, and are separate from the values the model carries internally.

10.4.1 Reducing and decarbonising steam

For in-situ bitumen, the game is steam. Solvent and hybrid thermal-solvent processes inject light hydrocarbons (often propane) in place of, or alongside, steam, cutting the steam needed per barrel and lowering carbon intensity by up to about 50% ( 2025). Where steam is still needed, the Steam service can switch from gas boilers to cogeneration, electric, hydrogen, or even nuclear steam — the choice that most determines an in-situ barrel’s footprint.

10.4.2 Carbon capture and methane control

Because the oil sands produce large, relatively concentrated CO₂ streams (from steam generation, upgrading, and hydrogen production), CCS is the central deep-decarbonisation lever, and the proposed Pathways Alliance network would capture CO₂ from a cluster of oil-sands facilities at large scale (Pathways Alliance 2024). The model represents this through the CCS-equipped steam, upgrading, and still-gas routes and the shared CCS service. Enhanced leak detection (ELDAR) cuts the sector’s fugitive and vented methane, often at net-negative cost.

10.5 Economics

Technology choice at every node follows the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4. Each technology carries a capital cost, operating cost, an output (per-barrel) basis, service requests for the steam, hydrogen, and engine energy it consumes, any recovered-gas credit, and availability and lifetime years; these are combined into a lifecycle cost and converted to market shares.

Petroleum-crude technologies are discounted at a high rate — about 35% at most nodes, with some at 20–40% in the current data. These values are behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rates rather than true financial costs of capital, capturing the payback horizons and risk typical of the industry; CIMS applies this rate throughout the lifecycle-cost calculation, as set out in the discount-rate discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4.

Emissions are tracked as CO₂ (chiefly from steam, upgrading, and engine combustion) and CH₄ (fugitive and vented, with the ELDAR and solvent routes carrying lower factors), and the CCS service applies a 90% removal factor to the equipped routes. Because in-situ carbon intensity is dominated by steam, the carbon price acts most powerfully through the Steam competition — making solvent processes, electric/hydrogen/nuclear steam, cogeneration, and CCS progressively more attractive as it rises — while methane controls add cheap, often profitable, abatement.

10.6 Regional coverage and differences

Six regions carry crude production, but it is highly concentrated:

  • Alberta dominates, with essentially all of the oil sands (mining, in-situ, and upgrading) plus conventional crude — and therefore most of the sector’s energy use and abatement opportunity.
  • Saskatchewan carries conventional heavy and light/medium crude.
  • The Atlantic region carries offshore light/medium production (Newfoundland).
  • British Columbia, Manitoba, and Ontario carry smaller conventional volumes.

Because the oil sands are so steam- and energy-intensive, Alberta holds the overwhelming majority of the sector’s decarbonisation challenge and opportunity.

10.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The petroleum-crude sub-model captures the essential structure — conventional and oil-sands routes, the dominant steam service with its full range of low-carbon options, and CCS — which makes it well suited to analysing oil-sands decarbonisation. Its main limitations are the loose coupling of upgrading hydrogen to the hydrogen sub-model, reliance on inherited steam-to-oil and cost assumptions, and the need to keep the solvent-SAGD and CCS options aligned with fast-moving industry projects.
