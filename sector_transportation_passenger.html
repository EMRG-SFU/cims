26  Transportation (Passenger)

Passenger transportation is the personal-mobility module of CIMS-Transportation (CIMS-T), a demand-side sub-model: it meets a demand for personal mobility (people moved, in passenger-kilometres) by competing transport modes and, within the dominant road mode, competing vehicle powertrains. The emphasis is on the structure of the service flow model: the mobility-demand nodes, the modes and vehicles that supply them, and the costs — financial and intangible — that drive the competition. The tree is region-agnostic. Passenger transport is distinctive in two ways: it is among Canada’s largest emitting sectors, and its technology choices are unusually shaped by consumer preferences (range, refuelling convenience, vehicle size, and power), which CIMS represents through explicit intangible costs alongside financial cost.

26.1 Scope

CIMS represents passenger mobility — urban and inter-city travel of people — driven by passenger-kilometre demand. It covers personal vehicles (cars and light trucks), urban transit (public bus and rapid transit), inter-city bus and rail, and inter-city air. The driving demand is projected exogenously and can itself respond to cost (the model can shift demand for mobility).

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere: freight movement is in Transportation (Freight); off-road vehicles are handled separately; the fuels (gasoline, diesel, electricity, hydrogen, biofuels) are supplied by the energy-supply sub-models, including Ethanol, Biodiesel, Hydrogen, and Electricity; and vehicle manufacturing is in Light Industrial. This chapter covers the use of vehicles to move people.

26.2 Real-world context

Transportation is responsible for roughly a quarter of Canada’s greenhouse-gas emissions, and light-duty passenger vehicles are the single largest part of it. The emissions come overwhelmingly from burning gasoline and diesel, with associated health costs estimated in the billions of dollars annually (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2023). Policy is now driving a rapid transition: Canada’s zero-emission-vehicle (ZEV) mandate requires 100% of new light-duty vehicle sales to be zero-emission by 2035, with interim targets of 20% by 2026 and 60% by 2030, where a ZEV is a battery-electric, plug-in-hybrid, or fuel-cell vehicle (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2023).

Two forces shape the transition in the model. First, vehicle electrification: battery-electric vehicles now cost roughly CAD 50,000–70,000 (entry models from ~$40,000) but have 66–77% lower fuel-and-maintenance costs than gasoline vehicles, and the gap is closing (Natural Resources Canada 2026). Second, mode shift and transit electrification: moving trips from single-occupant vehicles to higher-occupancy vehicles, transit, and active modes cuts energy per passenger-km, and electrifying transit buses cuts their emissions by around 93% ( 2025). But consumer choice is not driven by cost alone — range anxiety, refuelling availability, and preferences for larger, more powerful vehicles all matter, which is why CIMS-T carries intangible costs to reproduce observed behaviour. The structure captures all three levers: powertrain competition, mode competition, and the intangible-cost preferences that temper both.

26.3 Model structure

The sector divides into a Product sub-tree — the mobility demand by mode — and a Service sub-tree of the vehicles and transit systems that supply it. Figure 26.1 shows the structure. Each node carries a competition rule (see Chapter 4); mode and powertrain shares are determined by lifecycle cost plus intangible cost.

26.3.1 Products: mobility demand by mode

The Mode node splits personal mobility into:

  • Urban — single-occupant vehicle (SOV), high-occupant vehicle (HOV), public transit, and walk/cycle. The competition among these is the model’s representation of urban mode shift.
  • Intercity Land — passenger vehicle, bus, and rail.
  • Intercity Air — competing conventional, efficient, electric, and hydrogen aircraft.

Demand can shift between modes (SOV↔︎HOV↔︎transit↔︎active; vehicle↔︎bus↔︎rail) as relative costs change, one of the endogenous actions CIMS-T simulates.

26.3.2 Services: vehicles and transit

  • Passenger Vehicles — the size classes (small/large cars, small/large light trucks) — and Passenger Vehicle Motors, where the powertrains compete: gasoline (existing/standard/efficient), hybrid, plug-in hybrid, battery-electric (by range, e.g. 500 and 800 km), and hydrogen fuel-cell. Powertrain and efficiency are specified for new vehicles; older vintages are tracked and retired by a stock-turnover function.
  • Transit — Public Bus (diesel, hybrid, electric) and Rapid Transit.
  • Intercity Bus — diesel, gasoline, hybrid, biodiesel-hybrid, natural-gas, hydrogen.
  • Intercity Rail — diesel (and efficient diesel), electric, hydrogen, biodiesel-hybrid.

26.4.1 Electrifying the light-duty fleet

The dominant lever is electrifying personal vehicles. The model competes battery-electric (at different ranges), plug-in hybrid, hybrid, and fuel-cell powertrains against gasoline, with the ZEV transition emerging from their falling capital costs and much lower running costs — tempered by the intangible costs that capture range anxiety, charging/refuelling availability, and the consumer preference for larger vehicles. Because powertrain is set for new vehicles and the fleet turns over through retirement, the modelled transition is gradual even under a sales mandate.

26.4.2 Mode shift and transit/rail electrification

Beyond the vehicle, shifting trips from single-occupant cars to high-occupancy vehicles, public transit, and walking and cycling lowers energy per passenger-km — one of CIMS-T’s endogenous actions, governed by the intangible cost differences between modes. Electrifying transit buses (and, where lines justify it, rail) cuts those modes’ emissions by around 90% ( 2025), and the model competes electric and hydrogen buses and electric and hydrogen rail directly.

26.4.3 Decarbonising aviation

Inter-city air is the hardest to abate. The model already competes efficient, electric, and hydrogen aircraft (the last two viable mainly for short-haul), but the principal near-term route — sustainable aviation fuel and e-fuels — is not yet represented (see the TODO above).

26.5 Economics

Technology and mode choice follow the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4, but passenger transport relies more than any other sector on intangible costs — the non-financial component of the choice. CIMS-T’s intangible costs encode consumers’ revealed willingness to pay for vehicle size and power, their preference for the range and refuelling convenience of gasoline over alternative fuels, and their preferences among urban modes. These are calibrated from market data and stated-preference surveys, and they are what allow the model to reproduce observed adoption rather than the cost-minimising choice a purely financial calculation would predict.

Financial costs are discounted at behavioural rates — around 8% for some capital-intensive, long-lived assets (rail, transit infrastructure) and around 25% for personal-vehicle decisions in the current data. As elsewhere in CIMS these are behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rates rather than pure financial costs of capital; together with the intangible costs they govern technology choice, as set out in the discount-rate and intangible-cost discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4.

Emissions arise from fuel combustion in the vehicles, buses, trains, and aircraft, entering through each technology’s fuel service requests and the carbon price; battery-electric options have zero tailpipe emissions and inherit the carbon intensity of the grid. The emissions benefit of electrification therefore depends on regional grid intensity, and the benefit of biofuels on their life-cycle intensity from the Ethanol and Biodiesel sub-models. A note of caution carried from the original documentation: many of CIMS-T’s intangible-cost parameters are under active review against stated-preference survey data, so the modelled pace of the ZEV transition is sensitive to them.

26.6 Regional coverage and differences

All seven modelled regions carry the passenger-transport structure. Regional differences show up in urban form — denser regions (the large metropolitan areas) support more transit and active-mode share, while dispersed regions are more car-dependent — and in grid carbon intensity, which determines how much electrification actually reduces emissions (large in hydro-rich Québec, British Columbia, and Manitoba; smaller in Alberta and Saskatchewan until their grids decarbonise). Fuel availability (charging and hydrogen networks) also varies and enters through the intangible costs.

26.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The passenger-transport sub-model captures the essential structure — mobility demand, mode competition, powertrain competition, and a rich intangible-cost representation of consumer preference. Its main limitations are the absence of sustainable aviation fuels and e-fuels, the reliance on intangible-cost parameters that are still being recalibrated, the exogenous treatment of some transit-technology choices, and stock-turnover dynamics that make the modelled transition gradual. The TODOs above record the priorities, of which adding SAF/e-fuels and refreshing the intangible costs are the most important.
