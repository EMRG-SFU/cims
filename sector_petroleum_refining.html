11  Petroleum Refining

Petroleum refining is one of CIMS’s energy-supply and processing sub-models: it converts crude oil and bitumen into refined products — gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, fuel oils, petrochemical feedstocks, asphalt, lubricants, and coke. The emphasis is on the structure of the energy-service flow model: the process units that turn crude into products, the technologies that compete at each, and the energy services — above all process heat, steam, and hydrogen — that drive them. The tree is region-agnostic. The defining feature of refining is that almost all of its emissions come from burning fuel for process heat and steam (much of it the refinery’s own still gas) and from on-site hydrogen production, so its decarbonisation is about electrifying and decarbonising that energy and capturing the concentrated CO₂ streams — against a backdrop of long-run demand decline as transport electrifies.

11.1 Scope

CIMS represents the refining of crude oil and synthetic crude into finished products, through the standard process units: distillation (atmospheric and vacuum), cracking (coking and hydrocracking), hydrotreating (desulphurisation and sulphur recovery), reforming, alkylation, and isomerisation, plus by-products such as asphalt and lubes. The driving variable is refinery throughput/product output. Lubricating-oil, grease, and asphalt manufacture are included where carried out within refineries. The model carries the supporting energy services — direct process heat, steam, still gas, hydrogen, machine drive, and a CCS service.

Adjacent activities are handled elsewhere: crude oil and bitumen production (including oil-sands upgrading) is in Petroleum Crude; natural gas processing is in Natural Gas; petrochemicals made from refinery streams are in Chemical Products; and the combustion of the finished fuels is in the demand sectors. This chapter covers the refinery itself.

11.2 Real-world context

Canada has a modest fleet of refineries spread across the country, supplying gasoline, diesel, and other products to domestic and export markets. A refinery’s energy use and emissions depend on its complexity — the type of crude it runs and how much cracking, hydrotreating, and reforming it does to make high-value products. The bulk of refinery emissions come from burning fossil fuels for process heating and steam generation, with a further significant share from hydrogen manufacturing (steam methane reforming for hydrotreating and hydrocracking) (World Resources Institute 2023). Much of the process fuel is the refinery’s own still gas (off-gas from the process units).

The decarbonisation levers follow from that emissions structure, and Canada’s Roadmap for the Decarbonization of the Oil and Gas Sector names them directly: electrifying low- and medium-grade process heat, fuel-switching to low- and zero-carbon hydrogen for high-temperature heat, energy efficiency, and CCUS — and refining is regarded as one of the lower-cost CCS opportunities, with concentrated CO₂ available from the hydrogen plant and other units (Natural Resources Canada 2024; World Resources Institute 2023). Over the longer term, refinery throughput is expected to decline as road transport electrifies, shifting the product slate toward aviation, petrochemicals, and exports. The CIMS structure captures the decarbonisation levers as competing technologies at the heat, steam, still-gas, and hydrogen services, with a shared CCS service.

11.3 Model structure

The sector divides into a Product sub-tree — the refinery process chain — and a Service sub-tree of the energy services. Figure 11.1 shows the structure. Each node carries a competition rule (see Chapter 4); the process chain is largely fixed-ratio, and the technology choices occur in the energy services.

11.3.1 Products: the refinery process chain

The Refined Petroleum Products node holds the process units:

  • Petroleum Fractions — the main chain: distillation (atmospheric and vacuum) separates crude into fractions; cracked gas oil upgrades heavy fractions via coking and hydrocracking (at low, medium, and high conversion); hydrotreating (desulphurisation and sulphur recovery) cleans the products; and asphalt and lubes are by-product streams.
  • Alkylates — alkylation and isoparaffins (isomerisation), which make high-octane gasoline blendstocks.
  • Reforming — catalytic reforming for octane and aromatics (and a hydrogen by-product).

These units are the demand for the energy services; their relative throughput sets how much heat, steam, and hydrogen the refinery needs.

11.3.2 Services: heat, steam, still gas, hydrogen, and capture

  • Direct Heat — the process furnaces, competing natural-gas, heavy- and light-fuel-oil, LPG, and petcoke firing (with efficiency variants) and electric heaters (high and low efficiency). This is the central electrification lever for low- and medium-grade heat.
  • Steam — boiler- and cogeneration-raised steam, competing the full range of fuels (NG, oil, LPG, petcoke, with efficiency and CCS variants), electric and hydrogen steam, and gas cogeneration (CCGT/SCGT/back-pressure turbines, with and without CCS).
  • Still Gas — the refinery’s own off-gas, used in heaters (with efficiency and CCS variants) and boilers; the dominant self-generated fuel.
  • Hydrogen — on-site steam methane reforming (standard, efficient, and efficient + CCS for blue hydrogen), for hydrotreating and hydrocracking.
  • CCS — the shared capture service (90% capture), well suited to the concentrated CO₂ from the hydrogen plant and other units.
  • Machine Drive, HVAC, Lighting, Methane Blend — the shared utilities.

Refinery decarbonisation works through electrifying and decarbonising process heat and steam, low-carbon hydrogen, efficiency, and CCS on the concentrated streams. Table 11.1 summarises maturity and Table 11.2 gives indicative costs.

The estimates in Table 11.2 are order-of-magnitude figures from public sources (World Resources Institute 2023; Natural Resources Canada 2024), expressed in 2025 Canadian dollars (US sources converted at roughly 1.37 CAD/USD and adjusted to 2025). They are indicative, for relative comparison rather than budgeting, and are separate from the values the model carries internally.

11.4.1 Decarbonising process heat and steam

Process furnaces and boilers are the largest emission source, so the central lever is how their heat is supplied. The model competes electric heaters (viable for low- and medium-grade heat), hydrogen firing (for high-temperature duty that is hard to electrify), and efficiency-improved fossil firing, at the Direct Heat, Steam, and Still Gas services, alongside cogeneration. Electrifying the electrifiable heat and switching the rest to hydrogen is the backbone of a low-carbon refinery (World Resources Institute 2023).

11.4.2 Low-carbon hydrogen and CCS

Hydrogen manufacturing is the second-largest emission source. The model already competes blue hydrogen (SMR with CCS) at the Hydrogen service; linking it to the Hydrogen sub-model would also allow green (electrolytic) hydrogen. Because the hydrogen plant and several process units produce concentrated CO₂ streams, CCS is comparatively low-cost in refining, and the model routes the CCS-equipped heat, steam, still-gas, and hydrogen options to the shared capture service.

11.5 Economics

Technology choice at every node follows the lifecycle-cost competition of Chapter 4. Each technology carries a capital cost, operating cost, an output basis, service requests for the fuel, steam, hydrogen, and electricity it consumes, and availability and lifetime years; these are combined into a lifecycle cost and converted to market shares.

Petroleum-refining technologies are discounted at a high rate — 35% at most process nodes, with auxiliary and capital-intensive nodes at 40–50% in the current data. These values are behavioural (revealed or empirical) discount rates rather than true financial costs of capital, capturing the short payback horizons and risk aversion typical of the industry; CIMS applies this rate throughout the lifecycle-cost calculation, as set out in the discount-rate discussion of the Theory chapter and in Chapter 4. Heterogeneity (variance) parameters govern how sharply the energy-service competitions respond to cost.

Emissions are overwhelmingly combustion CO₂ from process heat, steam, still gas, and the hydrogen plant, entering through each technology’s fuel service requests and the carbon price; the CCS service applies a 90% removal factor to the equipped routes. Because so much of the fuel is self-generated still gas, the carbon price acts on the choice of how to supply heat and hydrogen — electrify, switch to hydrogen, or capture — rather than on a single dominant process emission. A structural consideration the model can represent is that refinery throughput is expected to decline over the long run as transport electrifies, which reduces absolute emissions independently of the per-barrel decarbonisation choices.

11.6 Regional coverage and differences

Six regions carry refining — Alberta, the Atlantic region, British Columbia, Ontario, Québec, and Saskatchewan — corresponding to where Canada’s refineries are located. Regional differences show up in refinery complexity and product slate, in the fuel mix available for process heat (cheaper gas in the West), and in grid carbon intensity, which determines how much electrifying process heat actually reduces emissions (large in hydro-rich Québec and British Columbia, smaller in Alberta and Saskatchewan until their grids decarbonise).

11.7 Limitations and planned extensions

The petroleum refining sub-model captures the process chain and, in detail, the energy services that dominate its emissions — direct heat, steam, still gas, and hydrogen — with a full set of electrification, hydrogen, cogeneration, and CCS options. Its main limitations are the older auxiliary structure (to be migrated to MECS machine drive), the on-site-only hydrogen representation (to be linked to the hydrogen sub-model), and reliance on inherited refinery-configuration and cost data. A broader consideration is the sector’s long-run decline: as transport electrifies, the most consequential question for refining emissions may be how fast throughput falls, which is driven by the demand sectors rather than within this sub-model.
